Detailed Analysis of "The Analects": For All Those Who Misinterpret Confucius (49)

2007/1/29 15:07:55

The Master said: Learning without thinking leads to bewilderment; thinking without learning leads to peril.

Yang Bojun: Confucius said: "Only reading without thinking leads to being deceived; only daydreaming without reading leads to lack of confidence."

Qian Mu: The Master said: "Merely learning from the outside without using thought will end in bewilderment. Merely using thought without learning from outside will end in peril."

Li Zehou: Confucius said: "Learning without thinking leads to bewilderment; thinking without learning leads to danger."

Detailed explanation: This is a chapter more thoroughly radical than Kant's "intuitions without concepts are blind, concepts without intuitions are empty." "Learning" (学) — hearing the "Way of the Sage," seeing the "Way of the Sage," "comparing against" the "Sage," constantly "calibrating" in real society — this takes difference as its premise. "Thinking" (思), as the Shuowen dictionary states, "思, means to contain (容)." "Containing" (容) cannot be separated from a certain paradigm — for example, a sieve does not "contain" water. And there is the concept of the "unthinkable" (不可思议), whose corresponding subtext is that "the thinkable" has an implicit standard; whatever exceeds this standard is deemed unthinkable. The premise of "thinking" lies in identity/sameness. For example, to be "thinkable" by Euclidean geometry, the subtext is that the rigid-body property of object movement possesses identity among the objects being "thought" about, which is why it is possible to treat all squares with equal side lengths as identical. Without "identity," all science would be unthinkable. Take physics — a simple example of identity is that in special relativity, all inertial reference frames are treated as identical. Without this requirement of identity, special relativity could not even get started. In philosophy, even Heidegger, who took it as his mission to reconstruct metaphysics, could not escape a certain subtext of "identity" in his "thinking" of Being.

Regarding "learning" premised on difference and "thinking" premised on identity, Confucius's insight shares something with Derrida. In Derrida's framework, difference and identity are entangled in différance, simultaneously present or absent. In différance, it is impossible to purely decompose or establish difference and identity, nor is it possible to construct or structure différance from difference and identity. This has nothing whatsoever to do with the nonsensical dialectics of "combining two into one" or "dividing one into two." Garbage propositions like "combining two into one" or "dividing one into two" could only be treated as treasures by philosophically illiterate quack philosophers.

The above three scholars and the usual interpretations all fail to understand what "learning" means and what "thinking" means, let alone the premises implicit in "learning" and "thinking." "Learning without thinking leads to bewilderment; thinking without learning leads to peril" is a classic example of mutual textual illumination (互文). Seeking the so-called "learning without thinking" or "thinking without learning" can only lead to bewilderment, weariness, and futility. "Learning" and "thinking" are entangled together in the present moment, inseparable. Any "learning" takes the "thinking" of identity as its premise — even Kant understood this clearly: without some presuppositional structure of "thinking," "learning" is impossible. And any "thinking" takes the "learning" of difference as its premise — this is something Kant could not have known. When Derrida says "I present myself to myself only after language," that begins to get closer to the mark.

Chán Zhōng Shuō Chán's Vernacular Direct Translation

子曰：学而不思则罔，思而不学则殆。

Confucius said: Separating the "learning" of difference from the "thinking" of identity can only lead to bewilderment, weariness, and futility.
