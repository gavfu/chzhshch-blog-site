Teaching You to Trade Stocks 46: Classification of Daily Movements

2007/4/18 15:36:09

I had originally planned to continue writing about the classification problem of trend types after the first hub forms, left over from the previous lesson. But I've discovered that too many people can't even figure out how to read daily charts, and this issue may be more urgent, so let me address it first.

Of course, if you're operating strictly at a certain level, how the day specifically unfolds is largely irrelevant — the trend won't be fundamentally different just because trading is organized by days. But classifying each day's movements is at least a useful auxiliary tool.

A day's trading is 4 hours, which means the system is composed of 8 thirty-minute K-lines. If we treat the overlapping portion of 3 adjacent 30-minute K-lines as a hub on the daily movement, then generally speaking, obviously, any day's movement falls into just three categories: I. Only one hub; II. Two hubs; III. No hub. Their strength increases in that order.

I. Only one hub

This is a typical balanced market. Generally, the first three 30-minute K-lines determine the entire day's fluctuation range. And the day's extreme positions — at least one of them — basically appear within these first three 30-minute K-lines, creating either the day's high or the day's low. Of course, this isn't absolutely certain, so this situation can be further sub-classified.

  1. The day's high appears within the first three 30-minute K-lines

This can be called a weak balanced market. The weakest of these closes at the day's low. Note, this has nothing to do with whether the day closes in the green or red — a gap-up open that still closes positive can form this weakest type of weak balanced market. Next weakest is closing below the hub; closing at the hub is a typical weak balanced market; closing near the day's high is the strongest type.

  1. The day's low appears within the first three 30-minute K-lines

This can be called a strong balanced market. The strongest closes at the day's high — again, whether it's a positive or negative close is irrelevant. Next strongest is closing above the hub; closing at the hub is a typical strong balanced market; closing near the day's low is the weakest type.

  1. Neither the day's high nor low appears within the first three 30-minute K-lines

This can be called a transitional balanced market. Its strength can similarly be defined by closing position, as described above.

Note, in all three situations above, the hub's position is not necessarily the overlap of the first three 30-minute K-lines — it can be formed by later K-lines.

II. Two hubs

Obviously, based on the directional relationship of the two hubs, this divides into upward and downward types. Generally, let's discuss the upward case; the downward case is simply the reverse.

Two hubs obviously cannot have overlapping areas — otherwise it would transform into the situation above. Therefore, the most distinctive feature of this pattern is that between the two hubs there is at least one 30-minute K-line, part of whose range doesn't belong to either hub. This range is called the unilateral zone, which is the most important characteristic of this type of movement and is the key position for subsequent movements. Note that the K-line containing the unilateral zone does not belong to either hub.

Since there are only 8 thirty-minute K-lines, based on where the unilateral zone is located, there are only two possibilities: 1. The unilateral zone is in the 4th thirty-minute K-line. 2. The unilateral zone is in the 5th thirty-minute K-line. This is why, for all unilateral movements, the reversal time always falls within the 30 minutes before or after the midday close.

Of course, the 4th and 5th thirty-minute K-lines can both simultaneously have unilateral zones. In the case where only the 4th K-line has a unilateral zone, the 8th K-line might produce a penetration of the unilateral zone. For example, yesterday's (2007/04/17) movement.

Obviously, for an upward case, the strongest is closing above the second hub; the weakest is when the 8th K-line penetrates the unilateral zone, ultimately closing below the first hub. Other strength levels can be determined sequentially based on closing positions.

III. No hub

This is the strongest unilateral movement. Among 8 K-lines, no 3 adjacent ones have overlapping portions. Once this occurs, it's a typical violent movement, and that day's K-line carries significant importance. Generally speaking, this kind of movement is very uncommon. For example, the February 27th movement was one. But don't think such a movement necessarily means the trend will continue — many false signals deliberately use this type of movement, especially when it occurs within a large daily-level hub, where false signals are even more likely. For example, February 27th. However, if this type of movement appears after a third-type buy or sell point, the probability of a major strong trend at a larger level is extremely high.
