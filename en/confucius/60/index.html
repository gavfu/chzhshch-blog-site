Detailed Analysis of "The Analects": For All Who Have Misinterpreted Confucius (60)

2007/3/26 15:19:01

子贡问君子。子曰：先行其言而后从之。

Yang Bojun: Zigong asked how to be a gentleman. Confucius said: "First practice what you intend to say, and then say it [that would be enough to qualify as a gentleman]."

Qian Mu: Zigong asked what makes a gentleman. The Master said: "A gentleman acts before he speaks, and then speaks in accordance with his actions."

Li Zehou: Zigong asked what is a gentleman. Confucius said: "First carry out what you say, then speak about it."

Detailed Explanation: Zigong, one of Confucius's most famous students, was intelligent and eloquent — precisely the type of person Confucius described in the previous chapter as "语之而不惰者" (one whose speech never weakens in debate). But in Confucius's heart, there was a distinction between him and Yan Hui, which is not difficult to discern from the relevant chapters in The Analects. In this chapter, Zigong asks about the standard for being a gentleman. In truth, this is a false question — the gentleman "hears, sees, learns, and practices" the "Way of the Sage," which is not something a static moral standard can define. Zigong hoped to obtain a fixed, definitional standard, but Confucius instead gave a present-moment response: "先行其言而后从之."

The interpretations of the three scholars above seem to suggest "action precedes speech," but what hasn't been spoken aloud is still "speech" — language is the home of existence, regardless of whether it is verbalized. This kind of logic that emphasizes whether something is said or not is the perfect breeding ground for hypocrites. In fact, "先行其言而后从之" is an abbreviation of "先行其言而后从其言." "行" (háng), to connect, to run through continuously; "言" (yán), not merely referring to discourse, but encompassing all of a person's thoughts and corresponding actions; "行其言," to continuously connect one's discourse, thoughts, and corresponding actions — which is what Confucius later expressed as "吾道一以贯之" (my Way is threaded through by one principle). Only through "行其言" can one achieve "一以贯之" (threading with one principle). "从" (cóng), through to "纵" (zòng), meaning extensive/broad. Merely "threading with one principle" without being able to "从之" (make it extensive) — without being able to spread it broadly — would be nothing more than a small clique's or even an individual's self-gratification, which has no real significance.

Chán Zhōng Shuō Chán's Vernacular Translation

子贡问君子。子曰：先行其言而后从之。

Zigong asked about being a gentleman. Confucius said: "First make one's discourse, thoughts, and corresponding actions continuously connected and threaded through as one, and then make this extensive."

子贡问曰："赐也何如？"子曰："女，器也。"曰："何器也？"曰："瑚琏也。"

Yang Bojun: Zigong asked: "What kind of person am I?" Confucius said: "You're like a certain vessel." Zigong said: "What vessel?" Confucius said: "A precious jade vessel used in the ancestral temple for holding grain offerings."

Qian Mu: Zigong asked: "What about me?" The Master said: "You are a kind of useful vessel." Zigong said: "What kind of vessel?" The Master said: "You are like the jade vessel used for holding grain offerings in the ancestral temple."

Li Zehou: Zigong asked Confucius: "How am I?" Confucius said: "You are a kind of vessel." "What kind of vessel?" "A sacred jade vessel for worshiping the gods."

Detailed Explanation: This chapter shows that Confucius was a person with quite a sense of humor. Zigong was clever but lacked wisdom, yet was quite self-satisfied with his cleverness, so he asked Confucius "What am I really like?" — hoping for Confucius's affirmation. "赐" (Cì) was Zigong's given name. Confucius gave a dual-meaning reminder to Zigong with "the gentleman is not a vessel" (君子不器) — but Zigong didn't understand and thought Confucius was calling him some kind of vessel, so he asked "what vessel?" Confucius, with great humor, told him: a 瑚琏 (hú lián) — a precious jade vessel used in the ancestral temple for holding grain offerings to the spirits.

Intellectuals and scholars have always had a tradition of preening. In truth, nothing is more useless than a scholar. The scholars' little games ultimately all become cannon fodder for greater political and economic games. Intellectuals either get used as weapons or waved as flags — or else they spend a hundred years as cold-door poor souls crying like shivering birds. Confucius's humor here is even more exquisite: a precious jade vessel for holding grain offerings in the ancestral temple — even if they enshrine you, you're still nothing more than an ornament on the altar. As for the "fame" that intellectuals love to compete for, it's merely like the quality of the jade in that ornamental vessel — no matter how fine the jade's quality, it's still just an ornament.

Chán Zhōng Shuō Chán's Vernacular Translation

子贡问曰："赐也何如？"子曰："女，器也。"曰："何器也？"曰："瑚琏也。"

Zigong asked: "What about me?" Confucius said: "You — a 'vessel.'" "What kind of vessel?" "A precious jade vessel like the one used for holding grain offerings in the ancestral temple."
